A still image can describe a shape beautifully. It says less about what happens when someone reaches for it. Movement introduces another set of questions: where does an action begin, how does the object respond, and what tells a person that the action is complete?

For the Ambient Dial cover, a small interactive model offers a way to explore some of those questions. Dragging changes the viewing angle. Adjusting a slider turns the model’s dial and changes the arc on its screen. The interaction is deliberately limited to something that can be understood in a few seconds.

## Choose one relationship

A prototype does not need to imitate every part of a finished product. This one focuses on the relationship between a rotary control and visible feedback. The important thing is that both changes belong to the same action.

The slider is a way to explore that relationship on a webpage. It does not reproduce the feeling of a physical dial. Treating that difference explicitly helps keep the discussion useful: we can talk about direction and range, while leaving resistance and tactility for a physical prototype.

The same restraint applies to the model. Its visible form is reconstructed with reference to the product image. The side and back are approximations. Rotating it helps explain volume, but does not establish manufacturing dimensions.

## Keep the prototype honest

Before adding another effect, ask what it would help someone understand. A small change in viewpoint may reveal the depth of the control. An elaborate camera move may simply make it harder to inspect.

The most useful next iteration might be quieter: a clearer starting position, a more readable response, or an easier way to reset. It might also be a piece of foam and a real knob, if the next question concerns the hand.

A prototype earns its place when it helps us decide what to do next. Its finish should support that conversation, with its limitations kept close enough to see.
